# NomNomzBot user-guide voice

The target voice for this project is set by the passages the owner chose under "Who the reader is". The owner chose B first and it still shows the connected, spoken wording we want, but its opening is superseded: see that section. Learn the decisions these passages make; do not copy their sentences or turn them into a page template. These examples guide writing and are not evidence that a new page passed a reader test.

## The nine failures to correct

1. Start from where this reader stands: they know bots and are new to this one. Do not announce a feature's capabilities or explain what the category is for.
2. Cut sentences about the page. State the useful information instead.
3. Avoid a manufactured rhythm of matching halves and lists of three. Connect related thoughts without counting them into a pattern.
4. Give the main path the most space. Access requirements and consequences that affect the steps go before them; unrelated details do not.
5. Recommend one starting option when the evidence supports it. Explain why it suits this reader's goal.
6. Explain terms when the reader first needs them. Delay words such as prefix, match and pipeline until they do useful work.
7. Say what to leave alone for now. Get one useful result working before introducing the other options.
8. End the main path with the observable moment it worked, rather than a summary of the instructions.
9. Keep the reader on the page until that first success. Link onward afterward, except where a real prerequisite must be learned elsewhere.

## Inventory to explanation

**Before — rejected A:**

> Chat commands let viewers ask for information or start an activity without waiting for you to notice the message. A schedule command can answer a question you hear every stream; a welcome command can greet the person who used it. You create both on the **Commands** page in the dashboard.

**After — the opening of chosen B:**

> Viewers ask the same things every stream, like when you're live next. A command answers for you. Someone types `!schedule`, and the bot replies with your times.

**Why:** The reader recognizes a recurring question and sees the chat interaction that answers it. The explanation earns its space through a useful situation instead of adding a second introduction above a catalog.

## Chopped to connected

This comparison uses the command-creation outcome already described by the guide.

**Before:**

> Choose **Create command**. The dialog closes. The editor opens. The command is switched on.

**After:**

> Choose **Create command**. The new command opens in the editor, already switched on.

**Why:** The related results form one recognizable state. The reader knows what should be on screen without listening to a separate sentence for each interface transition. Short sentences are useful; a sentence per tiny fact is not the target.

## Padded to focused

**Before — from Create a command:**

> For a schedule command, begin with a reply you can read back exactly as written. You can add changing information and more advanced actions after confirming that the simple version works.

**After:**

> Start with a plain reply containing your stream times. Leave the other options alone until the bot answers in chat.

**Why:** The recommendation, what to defer and the checkpoint are explicit. The reader can act without interpreting commentary about a simple version and later capabilities.

## Chosen B in full

> Viewers ask the same things every stream, like when you're live next. A command answers for you. Someone types `!schedule`, and the bot replies with your times.
>
> Let's make that one. Open **Commands**, choose **New command**, and type `schedule` as the **Name**. In **Reply**, write your stream times the way you'd say them in chat. Leave everything else alone for now, and choose **Create command**.
>
> Now type `!schedule` in your chat. The bot answers. You can test this while you're offline, as long as you're the broadcaster or a moderator.

This passage illustrates the voice. A task page must still establish its real setup, access requirements, current labels and defaults from evidence. Do not copy its omissions into another workflow. A reference page can answer directly without adopting this task-guide shape.

## Who the reader is

The owner, 9 November 2026: people looking for a stream bot were often recommended one and already know in general what a bot does. Explaining what a bot is for belongs on the bot's introduction page, never in a specific topic.

This supersedes chosen B's opening, which explains why a command is useful. That explanation goes on the introduction page only. Every topic page starts from how the feature works in this bot.

Knowing bots is not the same as being ready. The reader may not have connected this bot or have the right role, so real requirements still come before the steps. Do not let this become a dry transcript of clicks: keep the reasons for choices, causes and outcomes, and cut only the generic teaching.

**Before — explains what the reader already knows:**

> Viewers keep asking when you're live next. Make a command called `schedule`, and when someone types `!schedule`, the bot answers with your stream times.

**After — chosen by the owner:**

> Go to **Commands** and choose **New command**. Enter the name without the `!`, write your reply and save. It is on straight away.
>
> You can test it while you're offline, as the broadcaster or a platform moderator. Ordinary viewers only get an answer while you're live, unless you switch on **Keep commands and rewards on while offline**.

**Why:** The reader knows what a command is and is new to this bot. The words go to where things are here and to behavior they could not assume.

**Also chosen by the owner, for a settings topic:**

> Open the command and go to **Trigger and cooldowns**. **Chat cooldown** is one wait for everyone. **Per-person cooldown** is a separate wait for each viewer. Both are in seconds, and 0 means no wait.
>
> With a chat cooldown of 10 and a per-person cooldown of 60, viewers have to wait 10 seconds after someone uses the command, and that same person has to wait a full minute. A refused try doesn't restart either wait. You and your moderators skip these waits, so test with an ordinary viewer account.

**Why:** It names the two settings, says what each does in one line, then shows them with real numbers. The longer second-by-second timeline can wait lower on the page for readers who need it.

**Also chosen by the owner, for a feature with surprises:**

> A new timer starts as soon as you save it. The first line goes out within about 30 seconds, not after 30 minutes, and timers keep posting while you're offline, so switch a reminder off when your stream ends if it's only for broadcasts.
>
> Go to **Triggers and timers**, open **Timers** and choose **New timer**. Give it a name, put each message on its own line under **Messages**, and leave **Minutes between posts** at 30. The bot posts your lines in turn, one each time.

**Why:** The words go to the two things the reader would otherwise get wrong: when the first post happens, and that it does not stop by itself. Both come before the steps, because saving starts the posting. The owner chose this wording with these sentences after the steps; they were moved up for that reason.

## Listening and review

The owner uses read-aloud and wants wording that can be followed without rereading. Review the blind answers before showing length warnings. Then judge each warning by its cost to the reader; a number alone is not a defect. Agent review does not count as the owner's listening test or as testing by new streamers and moderators.
